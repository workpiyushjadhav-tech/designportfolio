Solving the group dining problem
that food halls created but nobody designed for

First, a definition

What is a food hall, exactly?

Not a restaurant, and not quite a food court either. A food hall is a shared room where several independent kitchens — a burger stall, a ramen counter, a cocktail bar — each run their own menu and their own till, but everyone at the table eats together, at shared, unassigned seating.

That single detail is where the whole story starts: one table, one group, one bill in everyone's head — but as many separate ordering systems as there are stalls.

Picture the room: four or five stalls around the edge, one shared floor of tables in the middle. Nobody owns the whole experience — so nobody designed for the whole experience either.

Research & Discovery

Five evidence areas. One structural gap.

Secondary research, competitive audit of 9 UK food halls, review mining across TripAdvisor, Reddit, Google, and Facebook. Zero competitors support multi-vendor group sessions without pre-setup.

Evidence 01

Group Dining Behaviour

Behavioural economics reveals a hidden cost to dining in groups that the current technology does nothing to address.

  • 37% more spending when splitting equally
  • Sequential ordering creates conformity pressure - first orderer sets unspoken spending ceiling
  • 62% report higher anxiety splitting with acquaintances vs close friends
  • 78% prefer paying only for what they ordered - but social cost prevents asking
Evidence 02

UK Food Hall QR Audit - The Gap

A review of 9 UK food halls found only 4 had any QR ordering capability. Of those, none supported multi-vendor group sessions.

  • The 4 with QR ordering covered one vendor or drinks only - never the whole hall
  • The other 5 were counter service, no QR at all
  • The gap is structural, not incidental - no product exists for this context
Evidence 03

Payment Splitting Psychology

The money conversation at dinner carries more social risk than most people realise - and more than most products account for.

  • 72% find discussing finances harder than death, religion, or politics
  • 3.2× more likely to overpay than risk perceived conflict
  • Simultaneously managing mental arithmetic + social impression creates genuine stress
  • The desire for fairness exists - the social architecture for it doesn't
1

The trough isn't where you'd expect.

I started this project assuming payment was the pain point. Mapping Sam's emotional journey said otherwise: the deepest drop is at ordering — the exact moment shown above, guessing for six people in WhatsApp while four have replied and two haven't.

Planning (confident) → Ordering (anxious) → At the Table (stressed) → Payment (resigned) → Aftermath (frustrated). That single insight reset the whole brief: solve coordination first, payment second.

From Insight to Brief

One design question. Everything else follows.

With ordering identified as the real anxiety point, the brief became: solve coordination first, payment second.

How Might We

How might we help groups coordinate orders, pay fairly, and enjoy the social experience - without any app, account, or designated payer?

2

Four ways to split a bill. One that actually respects Priya.

Buy a Feature (n=3 — directional, not conclusive) surfaced a real surprise: "Join session" scored 0/3 despite being classed Must Have, so the join flow got radically simpler.

Wireframes and Ideation

#3

One layout system. Every menu.

The hardest visual problem: make a burger stall, a ramen counter, and a cocktail bar feel like one coherent product, not a generic aggregator. Variety comes from photography and vendor colour headers; the underlying card structure never changes. Four core flows carry that system end to end — from creating the session through to tracking the food.

Key Screens - 4 Core Flows
Flow 01

Host: Create & Share Group Session

One tap to create a session after QR scan. 4-digit code, scannable QR, and shareable link - whichever works for the group.

  • No account creation - guest checkout from the start
  • Auto table number detection from QR metadata
  • Session closes when host submits - guests can't accidentally submit
Flow 02

Browse Accessible and Unified Multi-Vendor Menu

One component system across every vendor. Variety comes from photos and vendor colour headers - structure stays identical.

  • Cross-vendor dietary filter
  • Dietary tags include text labels alongside icons (WCAG 1.4.1)
  • Dyslexia-friendly font toggle available
Flow 03

Payment: Transparent Itemised Split

Defaults everyone to paying their own items. Equal split is a toggle, not the default.

  • Real-time "remaining table balance" shows group total settled
  • Apple Pay / Google Pay supported
  • Designed so one failed payment doesn't block others' confirmed orders (not yet tested against a real failure)
Flow 04

Track: Live Order Status Per Vendor

Live tracking per item, per vendor. Absent from every existing competitor in this context.

  • Each item tracked: Received - Preparing - Ready
  • Notification when all group items are ready
  • Scored highest in Q5 (functions well integrated) on SUS

The honest part

What held up, and what I'd still want to test.

What Worked

The journey-map reframe held. Reordering the brief around ordering, not payment, shaped every decision after it.

Submission control staying with the host. Guests control what they order, Sam controls when it's submitted — a clean separation that solved a real failure mode without adding friction.

One card component for every cuisine worked. Coherent without reading as a generic aggregator.

What Didn't / What's Unresolved

Dietary filter discoverability. Two of five participants failed this task entirely — a pure IA problem, not an iconography one.

Buy a Feature with only 3 participants. Feature 9 scoring 0/3 was compelling but, with three people, hard to call signal over noise. Needed six to eight to be defensible.

Untested in real conditions. All five participants were university peers with high digital literacy. Older diners, non-English speakers, and a genuinely noisy food hall are still untested.
